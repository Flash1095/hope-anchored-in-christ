Scribal Craft

Topic
Scribal Craft
Category
Custom
Summary
The scribal craft is the skilled, rule-bound work of copying and preserving the sacred scrolls by hand: the materials, the lettering, the counting, and the reverence with which a scroll was handled.
Jewish Background
A scribe, a sofer, wrote on parchment made from ritually clean animal skins, with a quill and specially prepared ink, following strict rules for letter forms, spacing, and line layout. Because no printing existed, accuracy was guarded by counting: scribes counted the letters and words of a book and knew its middle letter, so a copy could be checked against the count. A scroll with an error could be corrected within strict limits; a scroll beyond repair was not thrown away but buried, in the practice known as genizah, because it carried the divine name. The model scribe was Ezra, described as skilled in the Law of Moses, and the king was commanded to write his own copy of the Law and read from it all his life. This entry covers the craft only; the scribes as a class and party belong to the Groups and Factions entry on the Scribes.
Points To Christ
EXPLICIT New Testament link: Jesus said that not the smallest letter or stroke of a pen would pass from the Law until all was accomplished (Matthew 5:18). His word for the smallest letter, iota, and for the stroke, keraia, presupposes exactly the scribal world of counted letters and careful penmanship described here: the text he trusted was the text the scribes had guarded.
