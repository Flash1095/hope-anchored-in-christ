Rabbinic Interpretation Methods

Topic
Rabbinic Interpretation Methods
Category
History
Summary
Rabbinic interpretation methods are the disciplined ways Jewish teachers in the Second Temple period and after drew meaning from Scripture: Midrash, the four levels of Pardes, and the rules of Hillel.
Jewish Background
Midrash is interpretation that draws out what lies in a text, often by linking one passage to another through a shared word or theme. Pardes names four levels of reading: Peshat, the plain sense; Remez, the hinted or allusive sense; Derash, the applied or homiletical sense; and Sod, the hidden or mystical sense. Hillel the Elder, a leading teacher of the generation before Jesus, set out seven rules of inference, including qal va-chomer, arguing from the lesser to the greater, and gezerah shavah, linking two passages by a shared word. These methods were debated between the schools of Hillel and Shammai and shaped how the Law was applied to daily life. This entry covers the methods as a historical practice, not definitions of individual Hebrew words.
Points To Christ
EXPLICIT New Testament link: Paul argues in the manner of these teachers when he notes that the promise speaks of one seed, not many, and that the seed is Christ (Galatians 3:16), an argument turning on the singular form of a single word. On the road to Emmaus, Jesus himself interpreted Moses and all the Prophets in all the Scriptures concerning himself (Luke 24:27). TRADITIONAL reading: Messianic readers note that Jesus and Paul argued as rabbis of their day argued, using the same tools of close reading and passage-linking, while claiming an authority greater than the schools.
