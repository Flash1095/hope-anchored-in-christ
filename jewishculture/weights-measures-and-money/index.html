Weights, Measures and Money

Topic
Weights, Measures and Money
Category
Custom
Summary
Weights, measures, and money in Jewish life ran on shekels, talents, cubits, and baths, with honest scales commanded as a matter of justice, not just trade.
Jewish Background
The shekel was a weight before it was a coin — silver weighed out for land, for the Temple tax, and for redemption prices — and larger sums were counted in talents. Lengths were measured in cubits, roughly forearm to fingertip, and dry and liquid capacity in ephahs and baths. Torah commands just balances and honest weights, and the prophets denounce merchants who shrink the ephah and enlarge the shekel. In the New Testament period the denarius was a day's wage, the Temple used Tyrian silver, and the widow's two mites were the smallest copper coins in circulation.
Points To Christ
Jesus uses this everyday economy in his teaching — the labourers paid a denarius each in the vineyard, the widow whose two mites outweighed rich gifts — and he drives the money changers from the Temple courts, where commerce had crowded out prayer. No direct Messianic link is drawn for the units themselves; they are the honest background against which his teaching about wealth and worship is heard.
