What archaeological finds support the New Testament?

What archaeological finds support the New Testament?

Short Answer

Finds like the Pilate Stone, Caiaphas ossuary, Pool of Siloam, and Nazareth inscription confirm people and places named in the Gospels and Acts. They show the New Testament is rooted in verifiable first-century history.

Detailed Answer

The New Testament names officials, pools, synagogues, and towns, and archaeology keeps meeting it there. The Pilate Stone, found at Caesarea in 1961, is a first-century inscription naming Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judea, the very governor who sentenced Jesus. A decorated ossuary inscribed for Caiaphas matches the high priest of the trial narratives. Excavators uncovered the Pool of Siloam, where Jesus healed the man born blind, and the Pool of Bethesda with its five porticoes exactly as John describes. Gallio, the proconsul who heard Paul's case in Corinth, is fixed in time by the Delphi inscription, helping date Paul's ministry. First-century Nazareth houses confirm the small village existed when some claimed it did not in Jesus' day, and crucifixion remains, including a nail-pierced ankle bone, confirm Rome's brutal practice described in the Gospels. None of these stones preach the gospel by themselves. What they do is locate the gospel firmly in real soil, real politics, and real people, not mythic once-upon-a-time. The faith that anchors our hope claims events in verifiable history: a cross outside Jerusalem, an empty tomb, witnesses with names. Archaeology, again and again, finds that world exactly where the New Testament said it would be.
