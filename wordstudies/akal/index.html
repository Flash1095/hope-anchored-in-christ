ʼâkal

to eat (literally or figuratively)

Biblical Usage

One of the commonest verbs in the Bible — hundreds of times, from the first command in the garden (Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat) to the Passover lamb eaten in haste and the manna in the wilderness, where Israel learns man does not live by bread alone. The Aramaic form does the same work in Daniel and Ezra: Nebuchadnezzar, driven from men, did eat grass as oxen, and the king's officials tell Artaxerxes they eat the king's salt — literally, they have maintenance from the palace. Eating is where obedience and rebellion both begin, and in the Aramaic chapters it is humiliation, appetite, and loyalty.

Theological Significance

I hold that eating is the Bible's first theology lesson. God gives food freely, forbids one tree, and the whole Fall turns on a bite — and the whole redemption turns on another meal, the Lamb eaten in haste on the night of deliverance.
