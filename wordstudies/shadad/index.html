shâdad

properly, to be burly, i.e. (figuratively) powerful (passively, impregnable); by implication, to ravage

Biblical Usage

Shadad is the spoiler's work, laying waste to cities and fields until nothing is left standing. Isaiah pronounces woe on the spoiler who has not yet been spoiled, Jeremiah cries that destruction upon destruction is cried because the whole land is spoiled, and the LORD Himself says He has spoiled Babylon and destroyed her great voice. Micah hears the people take up the lament, we be utterly spoiled, he has changed the portion of my people.

Theological Significance

I believe the spoiler always meets a greater spoiler in the end, because God spoils the spoilers. The same word that falls on His people falls on their enemies too, and that is both the warning and the comfort.
