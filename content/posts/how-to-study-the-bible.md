You don't need a seminary degree to study the Bible well. You need a plan, a slower pace, and a willingness to let the passage say what *it* says — not what you already assumed it says. Here is a simple pattern you can use with any passage, starting today.

### 1. Pray first

Before you open the text, ask God to open your eyes to it. Study is not just an academic exercise; it is a conversation with the Author. A short prayer — "Lord, show me what you want me to see here" — sets the whole study right.

### 2. Read it in context

Context is king. Never build a belief on a single verse ripped out of its surroundings. Read the verses *around* your passage, then the whole chapter, and ask what book you are in and who wrote it to whom. A verse in a psalm of lament, a letter to a struggling church, and a prophecy to ancient Israel each need to be heard in their own setting first. Reading the verses around a passage keeps us from bending it to mean something it never said.

### 3. Slow down and observe

Read the passage twice, slowly. The second time, notice what you rushed past the first time: repeated words, contrasts ("but," "therefore"), commands, promises, and names. Ask simple questions of the text: Who is speaking? Who is listening? What happened just before this? What happens just after?

### 4. Ask three questions

Once you have observed, work through these three in order:

- **What does it say?** Stick to the words on the page.
- **What did it mean to the first hearers?** This is where context, culture, and the rest of Scripture help.
- **What does it mean for me?** Only after the first two. Application that skips interpretation is how verses get twisted.

### 5. Let Scripture interpret Scripture

If a passage puzzles you, look for cross-references — other places where the Bible speaks to the same theme. The Bible is one anchored story, and clearer passages shed light on harder ones. Follow the threads: a promise in Genesis fulfilled in the Gospels, a psalm quoted by Jesus, a command repeated in the letters.

### 6. Commentaries last, not first

Commentaries, study notes, and teachers are wonderful gifts — but they should *check* your study, not replace it. Wrestle with the passage yourself first, form your own careful reading, and then see what trusted teachers saw. You will learn far more, and you will be much harder to mislead.

### 7. Write it down

Keep a notebook or a note on your phone. Write the passage, one observation that surprised you, and one way you will live it this week. Writing turns reading into remembering, and remembering into obedience.

### Try it this week

Pick a short passage — say, Psalm 1 or Philippians 2:1–11 — and walk it through these seven steps over a few days. Don't rush. Soak in it, the way Psalm 1 describes the person who meditates on God's Word day and night: planted, steady, fruitful.
