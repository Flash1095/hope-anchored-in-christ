Dinah and Shechem

Story
Dinah and Shechem
Testament
Old Testament
Scripture
Genesis 34
Retelling
Dinah, the daughter of Leah whom she bore to Jacob, went out to see the daughters of the land, and Shechem, the son of Hamor the Hivite, prince of the country, seized her and violated her. Afterwards he loved her and spoke kindly to her, and asked his father Hamor to get her for him as his wife. Jacob heard what had happened, but held his peace until his sons came in from the field, and when they heard it they were grieved and very wroth, because Shechem had wrought folly in Israel, a thing that ought not to be done. Hamor came to Jacob and proposed that the families intermarry, dwell together, trade in the land and take possessions in it, and Shechem offered to give whatever dowry and gift they asked, if only he might have Dinah as his wife. The sons of Jacob answered deceitfully, saying they could not give their sister to one who was uncircumcised, but would consent if every male among Hamor's people were circumcised, and then the two peoples would become one. The words pleased Hamor and Shechem, who persuaded all the men of their city, and every male was circumcised. On the third day, when the men were sore, two of Jacob's sons, Simeon and Levi, Dinah's brothers, took their swords, came upon the city boldly, slew all the males, slew Hamor and Shechem, and took Dinah out of Shechem's house. The other sons of Jacob then spoiled the city, taking sheep, oxen, asses, wealth, little ones and wives captive, because their sister had been defiled. Jacob said to Simeon and Levi that they had troubled him and made him odious among the Canaanites and Perizzites, and that being few in number, his household might be destroyed. They answered, "Should he deal with our sister as with an harlot?"

Discussion
This is a painful chapter, and Scripture does not hide it: a grave wrong, a deceitful bargain, and revenge that went far beyond justice and endangered the whole family. God's people are shown here at their worst, which is why justice must never be taken into our own hands in anger. Reflect: 1) How do we tell the difference between seeking justice and taking revenge? 2) Where does anger, even righteous anger, need to be brought under God's control? 3) How should the church respond with care and truth when someone has been deeply wronged?

Significance
Dinah's story is a sober warning that wrongdoing breeds further wrongdoing: deceit and vengeance only deepened the family's trouble. It shows why God's people need justice that is truthful, measured, and entrusted to God rather than seized in anger.
