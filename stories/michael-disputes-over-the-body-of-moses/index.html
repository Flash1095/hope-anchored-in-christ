Michael Disputes over the Body of Moses

Story
Michael Disputes over the Body of Moses
Testament
New Testament
Scripture
Jude 9; Deuteronomy 34:5-6
Retelling
Jude writes a single verse about this matter, and Scripture narrates nothing more: Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil, disputed about the body of Moses, and durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee. Jude gives no setting, no cause for the dispute, and no outcome beyond Michael's words. What canonical Scripture does say about Moses' end is in Deuteronomy: Moses the servant of the Lord died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the Lord, and he buried him in a valley in the land of Moab, over against Bethpeor, but no man knoweth of his sepulchre unto this day. Later tradition links Jude's verse to a lost work known as the Assumption of Moses, in which Michael and the devil contend over Moses' body, but that work is not canonical Scripture, and Jude does not name it. This row therefore retells only what Jude and Deuteronomy actually say, and marks everything beyond that as tradition, not Scripture.
Discussion
What Scripture says here is very little, and what tradition adds is a great deal, so the two must be kept apart. Jude's point in context is about restraint: even the archangel, contending with the devil, did not bring a railing accusation but left judgment to the Lord, in contrast to those who speak evil of dignities. The verse is cited as an example of that restraint, not as a story to be expanded. Reflect: 1) Why does Jude use an example whose story he does not tell? 2) What does Michael's The Lord rebuke thee teach about contending without railing? 3) How do you handle a verse that points beyond canonical narrative without filling the gap from tradition?
Significance
Michael's dispute is preserved in a single verse whose restraint is its lesson: the archangel leaves judgment to the Lord rather than railing, and the rest of the story, if it exists anywhere, belongs to tradition, not to Scripture. Doubt: whether any fuller account of the dispute is historical at all, since no canonical narrative tells it, and the traditional source is a lost extra-canonical work.
