Onesimus Returns to Philemon

Story
Onesimus Returns to Philemon
Testament
New Testament
Scripture
Philemon 1:1-25
Retelling
Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ, and Timothy his brother, write unto Philemon, their dearly beloved and fellowlabourer, and to Apphia, and Archippus their fellowsoldier, and to the church in Philemon's house. Paul thanks God for Philemon's love and faith, and for the refreshing of the bowels of the saints by him. He writes that though he might be much bold in Christ to enjoin Philemon to do what is fitting, yet for love's sake he rather beseeches him, being such a one as Paul the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ. He beseeches Philemon for his son Onesimus, whom he hath begotten in his bonds, which in time past was to thee unprofitable, but now profitable to thee and to me. Onesimus Paul has sent again, and asks Philemon to receive him, that is, his own bowels, whom Paul would have retained with him, that in Philemon's stead he might have ministered unto him in the bonds of the gospel. But without Philemon's mind would Paul do nothing, that his benefit should not be as it were of necessity, but willingly. For perhaps, Paul writes, Onesimus therefore departed for a season, that thou shouldest receive him for ever, not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved, specially to me, but how much more unto thee, both in the flesh, and in the Lord. If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself. If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account; I Paul have written it with mine own hand, I will repay it, albeit I do not say to thee how thou owest unto me even thine own self besides. Yea, brother, let me have joy of thee in the Lord: refresh my bowels in the Lord. Having confidence in thy obedience, Paul writes, I wrote unto thee, knowing that thou wilt also do more than I say. He asks Philemon to prepare him also a lodging, for he trusts that through their prayers he shall be given unto them.
Discussion
The letter does not order Onesimus's fate; it asks. Paul sends back a man who was unprofitable and is now profitable, asks that he be received not as a servant but as a brother beloved, and offers to pay whatever is owed. The gospel remakes a household relationship without a single command about it. Reflect: 1) Why does Paul beseech rather than enjoin, when he could command? 2) What does it mean to receive someone for ever as a brother, not as a servant? 3) How does Paul's offer to pay model the way Christ bears what is owed?
Significance
Onesimus's return is the gospel applied to a household: a man who departed is sent back as a brother, a master is asked to receive him as Paul himself, and the church in Philemon's house is left to work out what brotherhood means in practice.
