The Siege of Samaria and the Four Lepers

Story
The Siege of Samaria and the Four Lepers
Testament
Old Testament
Scripture
2 Kings 6:24-33; 2 Kings 7:1-20
Retelling
Ben-hadad, king of Syria, gathered his whole host and besieged Samaria, and the famine inside became so severe that an ass's head sold for eighty pieces of silver and a measure of dove's dung for five, and women ate their own children. As the king of Israel passed on the wall, a woman cried to him for help over a bargain in which her son had been eaten and the other woman's son was hidden. The king tore his clothes, revealing sackcloth beneath, and swore to take Elisha's head that day, blaming the prophet for the siege. Elisha, sitting in his house with the elders, said the king's messenger was coming to take his head, and told them to shut the door against him. Then he prophesied that by the next day a measure of fine flour would sell for a shekel and two measures of barley for a shekel in the gate of Samaria. The king's officer scoffed, 'If the LORD would make windows in heaven, might this thing be?' Elisha answered that he would see it with his eyes but would not eat of it. That evening, four leprous men at the gate said to one another, 'Why sit we here until we die? If we go into the city, famine will kill us; if we sit here, we die; let us fall unto the host of the Syrians: if they save us alive, we shall live; if they kill us, we shall but die.' At twilight they went to the Syrian camp and found it empty: the LORD had made the Syrians hear the noise of chariots and horses and a great host, and they had fled, leaving tents, horses, asses, and all their provisions. The lepers ate, drank, and hid silver, gold, and clothing, then said, 'We do not well: this day is a day of good tidings, and we hold our peace.' They reported to the gate, the king sent horsemen to verify, and the people plundered the Syrian camp, so that flour and barley sold exactly as Elisha had said. The scoffing officer, set in charge of the gate, was trampled by the crowd and died, seeing the plenty but not eating of it.
Discussion
The siege of Samaria pairs the worst of famine with the best of good news, and the messengers are four outcasts whom the city had left outside its gate. Their decision, 'we do not well' to keep good tidings to ourselves, is the story's challenge. Reflect: 1) What good news are you keeping to yourself while others starve? 2) How does God use outcasts as his messengers? 3) What is the difference between the officer's scoffing and the lepers' desperate faith?
Significance
The siege of Samaria ended in sudden plenty when four lepers found the Syrian camp abandoned and carried the good tidings back to a starving city.
