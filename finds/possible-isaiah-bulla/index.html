Possible Isaiah Bulla

Find Name
Possible Isaiah Bulla
Description
A clay seal impression (bulla) from Iron Age IIB, palaeographically dated 750–675 BCE, recovered in 2009 during Eilat Mazar's Ophel excavations south of Jerusalem's Temple Mount, in an Iron Age structure called the 'Royal Building,' and recognised during wet-sieving at Emek Tzurim rather than in the field itself. First published by Mazar in February 2018 in Biblical Archaeology Review ('Is This the Prophet Isaiah's Signature?'), then in an official excavation report later in 2018. The fragmentary inscription, in three registers, reads 'l-yšʿyh' ('Belonging to Yešaʿyah[u]', i.e. Isaiah) with 'Nby' below, and the upper register carries traces of a grazing-doe motif, a Judahite blessing and protection symbol. This is a DIFFERENT artifact from the site's existing 'Seal of Isaiah' entry.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Disputed
Interpretations
Mazar proposed 'Belonging to Isaiah [the] prophet,' citing the bulla's proximity (about 3 metres) to the Hezekiah bulla and Isaiah's association with Hezekiah (2 Kings 19–20; Isaiah 37–39). The identification is challenged on four grounds, stated exactly: (a) the name Isaiah and its root yšʿ were common, with nearly twenty biblical bearers of yšʿ-based names; (b) there is no patronymic ('Isaiah son of Amoz') as customary on Hebrew bullae; (c) the crucial final aleph that would complete nbyʾ ('prophet') is NOT legible, and the damaged letter could be taw, teth or lamedh (yielding a personal name such as Nbyt), or Nby may be complete as a personal name (attested on a Lachish bulla) or a gentilic ('Nobite'); (d) Alan Millard noted a title would normally carry the definite article, and Lawrence Mykytiuk cautioned the bezel ring cannot be drawn precisely enough to prove room for an aleph. Mazar herself called the missing aleph an 'important obstacle.'

Biblical Significance
If it is the prophet's seal, it would be the first extra-biblical reference to Isaiah; even if it is another Isaiah's, it places the prophet's name and a royal-administration context in Hezekiah's Jerusalem.
