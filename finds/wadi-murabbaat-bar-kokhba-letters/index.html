Wadi Murabba'at Bar Kokhba Letters

Find Name
Wadi Murabba'at Bar Kokhba Letters
Description
Manuscripts and documents from the Bar Kokhba period found in caves in Wadi Murabba'at, which runs down to the Dead Sea from the west about 18 km south of Wadi Qumran and some 25 km southeast of Jerusalem. Bedouin offered inscribed fragments to the Palestine Archaeological Museum in October 1951, and a team led by G.L. Harding and Pere R. de Vaux explored four caves early in 1952, recovering a considerable quantity of manuscript material; another cave entered by shepherds in March 1955 yielded a scroll of the Twelve Minor Prophets. The caves' holdings include documents from the Bar Kokhba revolt of AD 132-135, when Jewish refugees hid their valuables and papers in Judean Desert caves. Not to be confused with the famous signed letters of Simon ben Kosiba from the Cave of Letters at Nahal Hever, excavated by Yigael Yadin in 1960-1961.
Historical Period
Roman
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The Murabba'at caves are post-biblical, but they illuminate the world that followed the New Testament: Jewish communities under Rome, still writing Hebrew and Aramaic, still keeping deeds and letters, and finally hiding them from Roman armies a generation after the Temple's fall. They also show why the Dead Sea region preserves documents at all: the desert's dry caves.
Alternative Names
Murabba'at Scrolls; Wadi Murabba'at Documents
Biblical Significance
The caves preserve the paper trail of Jewish life under Rome in the generation after AD 70, the world into which the early church carried the gospel.
