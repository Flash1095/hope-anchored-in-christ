Annals of Tiglath-Pileser III

Find Name
Annals of Tiglath-Pileser III
Description
The Annals of Tiglath-Pileser III are the year-by-year royal inscriptions of the Neo-Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III (reigned 745–727 BC), carved on stone wall slabs lining his Central Palace at Nimrud (ancient Kalhu). The annals survive only fragmentarily: a later king, Esarhaddon, dismantled the palace and reused many slabs, damaging, erasing or displacing them. The surviving texts were recovered in the 19th-century excavations at Nimrud, notably by Austen Henry Layard, and reconstructed from dispersed fragments in the modern scholarly edition RINAP volume 1. They record the king's campaigns in chronological order, including his western campaigns against Israel, Judah and their neighbours.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Direct
Interpretations
The annals are a primary Assyrian source for the reign of the king the Bible calls Pul (2 Kings 15:19) and Tiglath-Pileser (2 Kings 15:29; 16:7). They corroborate the tribute paid by Menahem of Samaria and the campaigns against Pekah, Hoshea and Rezin that frame Isaiah 7 and 2 Kings 15–16. Caution: royal annals are court rhetoric written to glorify the king, not neutral history, and the text survives only in reconstructed fragments, so individual readings can be uncertain. The annals must be distinguished from the king's summary inscriptions, which list the same events geographically rather than chronologically.

Biblical Significance
The annals give the Assyrian side of the events behind 2 Kings 15–16 and Isaiah 7: Tiglath-Pileser's tribute demands, the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, and the fall of Damascus and northern Israel. They confirm that the biblical kings Menahem, Pekah and Hoshea were real rulers dealing with a real Assyrian overlord.
