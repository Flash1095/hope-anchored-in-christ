Nabonidus Harran Stele

Find Name
Nabonidus Harran Stele
Description
Two rounded-top basalt steles of King Nabonidus (555-539 BC), later reused in building the Great Mosque at Harran. Each stele shows the king standing before the symbols of the moon-god Sin, the sun-god Shamash, and the planet Venus (Ishtar). The three-column Akkadian text explains Nabonidus's ten-year stay in Tema in Arabia and records that, after returning to Babylon, he rebuilt Ehulhul, the temple of Sin at Harran, and restored its divine statues. The inscription was composed after Tashritu (month VII) of his 13th regnal year (543 BC), most likely in his 14th or 15th year (542-541 BC).
Historical Period
Neo-Babylonian Period (626-539 BC)
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The stele is Nabonidus's own account of why he left Babylon for a decade, leaving his son Belshazzar in charge - the political background to Daniel 5, where Belshazzar, not Nabonidus, hosts the feast on the night Babylon falls. It also shows the king promoting Sin above Marduk, which helps explain the Babylonian priesthood's alienation from him. The text is royal self-justification, not a neutral history, so its claims about the people's sins should be read as propaganda.

Biblical Significance
Daniel 5 names Belshazzar as the king feasting in Babylon on the night of its fall; the Harran Stele explains why Belshazzar, rather than his father Nabonidus, was ruling in the capital - Nabonidus was away in Arabia for ten years.
