Tale of Two Brothers Papyrus

An ancient Egyptian folktale preserved on Papyrus D'Orbiney (P. Brit. Mus. 10183), written toward the end of the Nineteenth Dynasty by the scribe Ennana, in the reign of Seti II (c. 1200-1194 BC), and acquired by the British Museum in 1857 from Elizabeth d'Orbiney. The story follows two brothers, Anpu and Bata: Anpu's wife tries to seduce Bata, he refuses, she falsely accuses him of attacking her, and Anpu expels him; the truth comes out, the brothers are reconciled, and Bata's adventures continue through death, transformation, and rebirth until he becomes king.

Interpretations

The opening episode closely echoes Genesis 39, where Potiphar's wife accuses Joseph after he refuses her: a wife's false accusation after a refused seduction. Scholars note the parallel but stress that the relation between the two texts is unclear, if there even is one; the Egyptian tale is a folktale with gods, transformations, and a magical tree, not a historical source. It shows the motif was already old in Egypt when Genesis was written, which makes the Joseph story culturally plausible, but it is a parallel, not proof.

Scripture Illuminated

Genesis 39:1-20

Biblical Significance

Its false-accusation episode is the closest Egyptian parallel to Joseph and Potiphar's wife, showing that Genesis 39 uses a story pattern already familiar in Egypt.
