Beersheba Four-Horned Altar

Find Name
Beersheba Four-Horned Altar
Description
A large sacrificial altar of cut calcareous sandstone ashlars with chiselled horns on four corners, reconstructed to over 5 feet high (about 3 cubits, matching Exodus 27:1–2). Its stones were found in 1973 by Yohanan Aharoni at Tel Beersheba, reused in a storehouse wall in secondary use. The original is now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem, and a reconstructed replica stands at the Tel Beersheba national park entrance. Burn marks show sacrifices were performed on it.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
Two debates must be stated. First, when it was dismantled: Aharoni's 1973 press release linked the dismantling to Hezekiah's reform (2 Kings 18:1–4), while Yigael Yadin suggested it could be the altar of 2 Kings 23:8 destroyed by Josiah, and pottery from the dismantling layer has been dated 640–620 BCE, which points toward Josiah; most scholars now favour Hezekiah, but the question is not settled. Second, whom it served: its finely cut ashlars contrast with the unhewn stones of the Arad altar and with Deuteronomy 27:6's whole-stone rule, and one source suggests it may have served a pagan cult under kings such as Ahaz rather than Yahweh worship. Amos denounces worship at Beersheba (Amos 8:14).

Biblical Significance
It is the largest horned altar found in Israel and matches the tabernacle altar's dimensions in Exodus, while its deliberate dismantling illustrates the cultic reforms that centralised worship in Jerusalem.
