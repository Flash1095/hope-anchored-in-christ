Aramaic Levi Document

A Jewish pseudepigraphon, not canonical Scripture, composed in Aramaic and dated by most scholars to the 3rd century BC, with some arguing for the early 2nd century BC. It was first known from two Cairo Geniza fragments, Cambridge T-S 16.94 published in 1900 and Bodleian Ms Heb c 27 f 56 published in 1907, and seven copies have since been identified among the Dead Sea Scrolls, one from Cave 1 and six from Cave 4 (1Q21; 4Q213-214b); Greek extracts also survive in a 10th-century Athos manuscript. Put in the mouth of Levi, son of Jacob, it recounts his prayer, the Dinah episode and the slaughter of the Shechemites, dream-visions, Jacob ordaining Levi to the priesthood, Isaac instructing Levi in marriage and sacrificial law, the births and marriages of Levi's children, a poetic exhortation to learn wisdom and the scribal arts, and a warning about his descendants. It served as a source for Jubilees and for the Greek Testament of Levi, and it teaches that priesthood is greater than kingdom.

Interpretations

The document must be presented for what it is: a Second Temple retelling of Levi's life, not Scripture and not history in the modern sense, valuable because it shows how Jews of the 3rd century BC understood the priesthood, the calendar and Levi's role. Its seven Qumran copies show the Qumran community prized it highly. Readers should not quote it as Bible, but it illuminates the priestly worldview that shaped Judaism between the Testaments.

Scripture Illuminated

Genesis 34:25

Biblical Significance

A Second Temple retelling of Levi's life shows how the patriarch's priesthood was understood in the centuries between the Testaments, retelling the Dinah episode of Genesis 34 as the founding moment of Levi's priestly line.
