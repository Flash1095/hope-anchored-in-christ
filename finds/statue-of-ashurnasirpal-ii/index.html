Statue of Ashurnasirpal II

Find Name
Statue of Ashurnasirpal II
Description
A rare Assyrian statue in the round, carved from magnesite and set on a reddish-stone pedestal, 113 centimeters high, dating to the reign of Ashurnasirpal II (883-859 BC). Found in 1850 by Austen Henry Layard in the Temple of Ishtar Sharrat-niphi at Nimrud, it has been in the British Museum since 1851 (ME 118871). The king holds a sickle in his right hand and a mace in his left, symbols of his role as vice-regent of the god Ashur; an eight-line inscription across his chest gives his titles and genealogy and a campaign from the Tigris to Lebanon and the Great Sea (the Mediterranean).
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The statue is one of very few free-standing Assyrian royal statues, and its regalia - sickle and mace - visualizes the ideology of Assyrian kingship: the king as the god's deputy, harvesting enemies and wielding authority. It illuminates the imperial power Isaiah calls the 'rod of anger' in Isaiah 10, but it depicts Ashurnasirpal II, who reigned two centuries before Isaiah's Assyrian crisis, so it is background to the prophetic image, not its subject.

Biblical Significance
It puts a face and regalia to the Assyrian kingship that Isaiah later describes as God's instrument of judgment, showing the ideology behind the empire's claims.
