Enuma Elish Creation Tablets

Find Name
Enuma Elish Creation Tablets
Description
The Babylonian creation epic, named from its opening words 'When on high,' about a thousand lines on seven cuneiform tablets in Old Babylonian, recited each year on the fourth day of the Akitu (New Year) festival. Recovered by Austen Henry Layard in 1849 in fragmentary form in the ruined Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh and published by George Smith in 1876, it tells how the gods Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water) gave birth to the gods, how Marduk defeated Tiamat and split her body to form heaven and earth, and how humankind was created from a slain god's blood to serve the gods. Most of Tablet V has never been recovered.
Historical Period
Old Babylonian composition; Neo-Assyrian copies
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
Enuma Elish is a PARALLEL to Genesis 1, not proof of it and not its source in any simple sense: both begin with watery chaos, a dividing of waters, and a divine rest after creation, and both have the gods consulting before making man ('Let us make man,' Genesis 1:26). But the differences are decisive: Genesis has one God creating by word alone, without battle, without gods, and with humanity made in God's image for dominion, not as slaves to feed the gods. The epic's real value is showing what Israel's neighbors believed, which throws Genesis' distinctiveness into relief. Claims that Genesis was 'based on' the Babylonian myth overstate the evidence.

Biblical Significance
It is the great Babylonian creation story against which Genesis 1 must be read: the contrasts (one God, creation by word, humanity in God's image) are as instructive as the shared imagery of waters and divine rest.
