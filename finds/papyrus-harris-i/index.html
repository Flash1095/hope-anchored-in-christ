Papyrus Harris I

Find Name
Papyrus Harris I
Description
The Great Harris Papyrus, at 41 metres (136 feet) the longest known papyrus from Egypt, with about 1,500 lines of hieratic text (Papyrus British Museum EA 9999). Composed under Ramesses IV (c. 1152 BC) as a memorial to his father Ramesses III (1186-1155 BC), its first sections list the king's colossal donations to the temples of Thebes, Heliopolis, and Memphis, including 309,950 sacks of grain for Thebes alone, and its historical section summarizes the reign: wars against the Sea Peoples, the subjugation of the Edomites, Libyan wars, an expedition to Punt, copper from Atika, and a Sinai expedition. Found in 1855 in a cache near Medinet Habu and bought by Anthony Harris, it is now in the British Museum.
Historical Period
New Kingdom, Twentieth Dynasty
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The papyrus is a royal memorial, so its historical section is idealized and designed to glorify the king; its claims (hundreds of thousands of captives, total subjugation of the Sea Peoples) should be read as propaganda, not census figures. Its value is as a contemporary witness to the Sea Peoples crisis that reshaped the Levant at the end of the Bronze Age, the world into which the Philistines settled and Israel emerged.

Biblical Significance
It is the fullest Egyptian account of the Sea Peoples invasions whose aftermath produced the Philistine cities that dominate the books of Judges and Samuel.
