Khirbet Beit Lei Tomb Inscriptions

Find Name
Khirbet Beit Lei Tomb Inscriptions
Description
Ancient Hebrew graffiti in paleo-Hebrew script carved into the soft chalk walls of a multi-chamber Iron Age II rock-cut burial cave (bench tomb, typical of pre-exilic Judah) on the eastern slope of Khirbet Beit Lei, 5.5 km southeast of Beit Guvrin, 8 km east of Tel Lachish, about 20 miles southwest of Jerusalem. Accidentally exposed in 1961 during road construction and excavated in 1963 by Israel's Department of Antiquities under Joseph Naveh. Eight skeletons lay on limestone ledges, with a ring, a bronze earring and a bronze plaque, and the walls carry drawings of human figures (a possible lyre-player, a praying figure, a headdressed figure), two sailing vessels and a possible encampment or tent. The inscribed wall was cut out for preservation.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The longest graffito (Inscription A, at eye level) is reconstructed two ways: Naveh read 'Yahweh is the God of the whole earth; the mountains of Judah belong to him, to the God of Jerusalem,' while Frank Moore Cross read 'I am Yahweh thy God: I will accept the cities of Judah and will redeem Jerusalem.' Other graffiti plead 'Absolve (us) O merciful God! Absolve (us) O Yahweh!' and 'Deliver (us) O Lord!' Dating is debated: Naveh favoured the 8th–6th centuries BCE (preferring earlier), Cross no earlier than the 6th century, and a 2016 challenge (Mandell and Smoak) reads the graffiti in funerary and protective context rather than as crisis-refugee writing. Naveh tentatively linked them to refugees of Sennacherib's invasion and cautiously suggested a Levite-singer family tomb (from the lyre figure). The cave became known as the 'Jerusalem Cave' for its old-Hebrew 'Yerushalem' spelling. Later 'Lehi Cave' associations with the Book of Mormon (Joseph Ginat's 1971 BYU presentation equating Beit Lei with Samson's Lehi, Judges 15:9–19, and later LDS 'Beit Lehi' claims) are NOT supported by archaeology and must not be presented as the site's meaning.
Alternative Names
Beit Lei Inscriptions
Biblical Significance
The graffiti are rare personal prayers to Yahweh as God of Jerusalem, written on a tomb wall in Judah, showing lived faith outside the temple in the monarchy period.
