Eridu Genesis Tablet

The Eridu Genesis is a fragmentary Sumerian creation-and-flood tablet (CBS 10673) excavated at Nippur by the University of Pennsylvania expedition in 1893, first recognized as a creation-flood narrative by Arno Poebel in 1912 and published in 1914. Reconstructed and named by Thorkild Jacobsen in 1981, it dates to about 1600 BC. It tells how the gods created the 'black-headed people,' kingship descended from heaven, the first cities were founded (Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar, Shuruppak), the gods decreed a flood, and Ziusudra survived in a boat, sacrificed, and was granted eternal life in Dilmun.

Interpretations

The Eridu Genesis is the oldest surviving Mesopotamian flood composition in this tradition, and its creation-cities-flood sequence parallels Genesis 1-11 - but it is badly damaged, with large gaps reconstructed from context, and it is a parallel, not proof of the biblical account. Its gods create people as laborers, decree the flood in assembly, and reward the survivor with immortality, while Genesis presents one Creator, a flood as judgment on wickedness, and Noah as the head of a new humanity under covenant.

Scripture Illuminated

Genesis 1:1-2:3; Genesis 6:5-8:22

Biblical Significance

As the earliest Mesopotamian creation-flood text, the Eridu Genesis shows how ancient the creation-flood storyline is, and throws Genesis's distinctive claims about one God and human purpose into relief.
