Royal Steward Tomb Inscription

Find Name
Royal Steward Tomb Inscription
Description
A limestone lintel inscription in paleo-Hebrew from a rock-cut tomb in Silwan (Siloam), across the Kidron Valley from the City of David, Jerusalem. Discovered in 1870 by Charles Clermont-Ganneau; the inscriptions were cut from the tomb facade to preserve them and are now in the British Museum. The text resisted decipherment for about 80 years until Avigad's reading in 1953: 'This is [the sepulchre of ...]yahu who is over the house. There is no silver and no gold here, but [his bones] and the bones of his amah [maidservant or lady] with him. Cursed be the man who will open this!' Dated to the 7th century BCE.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
Only '-yahu' of the name survives, so the identification with Shebna rests on Avigad's reconstruction 'Shebnayahu' plus the unique title 'who is over the house' (asher al ha-bayith, the royal steward, the king's chief minister), matching Isaiah 22:15–25, where Isaiah rebukes Shebna, 'who is over the house,' for hewing himself a tomb on high. The identification is widely accepted but conjectural, not certain, which is why this entry is classed Contextual rather than Direct. Note also that Shebna may or may not be the same man as 'Shebna the scribe' of 2 Kings 18:18, 26, 37 and 19:2; Easton's treats them as different people.

Biblical Significance
It is the material counterpart of Isaiah's rebuke of a proud royal steward: a grand tomb, built high across the valley from the city, whose owner warns robbers that it holds no treasure, only bones.
