Temple Scroll (11Q19)

The longest Dead Sea Scroll, just over 8 metres long, presenting temple law, ritual and festivals as divine revelation, mostly as a close paraphrase of Deuteronomy 12–22 with the author's halakhic changes. The 11Q19 copy is dated paleographically to the end of the 1st century BC–beginning of the 1st century AD; it had been hidden for 11 years under a Bethlehem antiquities dealer's floorboards before Yigael Yadin acquired it in 1967 and published it in 1977. Five further associated manuscripts exist, the oldest (4Q524) dating to about 150–125 BC, and proposed dates for the composition range from the 5th century BC to the early 1st century BC.

Interpretations

The Temple Scroll shows a Second Temple author rewriting Torah law to resolve legal conflicts and describe an ideal temple, treating his own composition as revelation. Because it lacks key sectarian terminology and its oldest copy predates Qumran, it may come from broader Second Temple circles rather than the Qumran sect alone, so it must not be assumed to be the sect's central law book. Its scale and detail make it the fullest ancient Jewish temple plan outside the Bible.

Scripture Illuminated

Deuteronomy 12:1-14; Exodus 25:8-9

Biblical Significance

It shows how seriously Second Temple Jews took the Torah's temple laws, reworking them into a vision of perfect worship that helps explain the hopes behind the Jerusalem temple itself.
