Mesad Hashavyahu Ostracon

Find Name
Mesad Hashavyahu Ostracon
Description
An inscribed potsherd (ostracon), catalogued as KAI 200, about 20 cm high by 16.5 cm wide with 14 visible lines of text in ink, discovered in 1960 by Joseph Naveh at the Iron Age fortress of Mesad Hashavyahu near Yavne-Yam, on the Mediterranean coast between Tel Aviv and Ashkelon. It was found under the floor of a room adjacent to the guardhouse and gate complex, and it is now in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem. The text is a written appeal by a field worker to the fortress governor: after finishing his reaping and storing the grain 'before Sabbath,' his overseer Hoshayahu son of Shobai confiscated his garment, and the worker asks for its return, with his fellow reapers as witnesses. The fortress was abandoned about 609 BCE, after Josiah's death.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The letter is written in fine biblical Hebrew by a trained scribe (the worker probably dictated its substance), and the fortress's Hebrew ostraca show it was a Judahite outpost, likely under a military governor placed by Josiah. The garment (beged) was the worker's blanket at night and, for the poor, his only possession of value, which is why Deuteronomy 24:12–13 and Exodus 22:26–27 require a pledged garment to be returned by sunset. One nuance matters: the worker argues he met his quota and had no debt, rather than explicitly invoking the biblical law, so the letter shows the law's world, not a citation of it. The letter also bears the first known extra-biblical reference to the Hebrew Sabbath.
Alternative Names
Yavne-Yam Ostracon; Harvester's Letter
Related Books
Exodus; Deuteronomy
Scripture Illuminated
Exodus 22:26-27; Deuteronomy 24:12-13
Biblical Significance
It is a poor labourer's own voice appealing for justice under the cloak-pledge laws of Exodus and Deuteronomy, showing those laws were live social expectations, not just scribal theory.
