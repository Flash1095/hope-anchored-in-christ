Tel Rehov Apiary

Find Name
Tel Rehov Apiary
Description
The oldest excavated apiary in the ancient Near East, found in the centre of Iron Age Tel Rehov, one of the largest Iron Age cities in Israel. About 30 unfired clay cylinder hives survived in three rows and at least three tiers, in a walled courtyard; the excavators estimate the original apiary held 100-200 hives, yielding perhaps 500 kg of honey and 70 kg of beeswax a year. Each cylinder, about 80 cm long, had a small flight hole at one end and a removable lid at the other. Charred honeycomb inside held bees closest to the Anatolian honeybee, suggesting imported stock. The city was destroyed by a fierce fire in the 9th century BC, probably in an Aramean attack.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class
Contextual
Interpretations
The identification of the cylinders as beehives is confirmed by beeswax residue, charred comb and bee remains, and by Egyptian pictorial parallels. The Anatolian origin of the bees is a proposed identification from morphology, not a proven import record, and the attribution of the city's destruction to Hazael of Damascus is probable, not certain. No claim is made that this apiary belonged to any named biblical person.

Biblical Significance
'A land flowing with milk and honey' was not only poetry: at Rehov, honey was an industry, run at scale, with imported bees, in a city of the divided-monarchy period. The apiary makes the Bible's honeyed-land language concrete, and its wax mattered beyond food — beeswax was used in metal casting in the Jordan Valley, linking this yard to the bronze and copper work of the same region.
