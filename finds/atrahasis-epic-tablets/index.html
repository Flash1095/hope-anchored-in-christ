Atrahasis Epic Tablets

The Atrahasis epic is an Akkadian flood and creation story preserved on three tablets of about 1,000 lines, telling how the gods created humans to do their labor, then sent plague, famine, drought, and finally a flood because human noise disturbed the god Enlil. The oldest surviving copy dates to the reign of Ammi-Saduqa of Babylon (1647-1626 BC), about 1635 BC; its best-known tablet (British Museum 1889,0426.236) was excavated at Sippar, Iraq, with the first fragments found in 1876. Warned by the god Enki, the hero Atrahasis builds a boat and survives the flood.

Interpretations

Atrahasis is the closest Mesopotamian parallel to the Genesis flood and creation accounts, and it later fed into the Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI - but it is a parallel, not proof of Noah's flood. Its theology is the opposite of Genesis: many quarreling gods, a flood sent because humans were too noisy, and a hero saved by a god's trick. Comparing the two shows what is distinctive about Genesis: one sovereign God, a flood sent because of human wickedness, and a covenant afterward.

Scripture Illuminated

Genesis 1:26-27; Genesis 2:7; Genesis 6:5-8:22

Biblical Significance

Atrahasis shows that flood and creation stories circulated widely in ancient Mesopotamia, and highlights by contrast the Bible's unique picture of one just God judging sin and preserving a remnant.
