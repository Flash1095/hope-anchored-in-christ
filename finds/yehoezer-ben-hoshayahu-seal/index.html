Yeho'ezer Ben Hosh'ayahu Seal

Find Name
Yeho'ezer Ben Hosh'ayahu Seal
Description
A black stone seal announced in 2024, found in Israel Antiquities Authority and City of David excavations along the southern wall of the Temple Mount, in First Temple-period debris. Its centre is a finely carved winged figure in Assyrian style, a protective 'genie' of the kind known from Neo-Assyrian art of the 9th-7th centuries BC and the first such figure found in Israeli and regional archaeology, framed by a paleo-Hebrew inscription reading 'Belonging to Yehoezer, son of Hoshayahu'. A hole drilled through its length let it be worn on a chain, so it served both as a personal seal and as an amulet. Researchers believe the seal began as the amulet of Hoshayahu, a senior official of the kingdom of Judah, and that his son Yeho'ezer inherited it and added both names. The seal is dated to about 700 BC.
Historical Period
Iron Age II
Evidence Class

Interpretations
The seal is a window into Judah as an Assyrian vassal: a Judahite administrator wore an Assyrian protective figure, made by a local craftsman at a high artistic level, as his mark of authority. Its names are parallels, not identifications: Yeho'ezer resembles Yo'ezer, one of David's warriors (1 Chronicles 12:7), and Hoshayahu resembles Azariah ben Hoshaiah of Jeremiah 43:2, but neither match is the seal's owner, and the entry claims no biblical identification for either man.

Biblical Significance
The seal shows the Yahwistic naming, the administration and the Assyrian artistic influence of Judah's elite in the century of Isaiah and Hezekiah, without claiming a biblical identity it cannot support.
