Who Wrote About Moses' Death?

Deuteronomy ends with Moses' death, his burial by God, and a survey of his whole life: 'there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses.' If Moses wrote Deuteronomy, he cannot have written the account of his own death and its aftermath. The passage is often taken as proof that Moses did not write the Pentateuch at all, and that the traditional ascription of the books to him collapses.

Explanations

The widely accepted resolution is modest: the bulk of Deuteronomy can be Moses' teaching while its final chapter is a postscript added after his death, traditionally ascribed to Joshua, who succeeds him in the very next book. Ancient books did not carry the modern expectation that every word, including a closing obituary, must come from the named author's hand; a disciple or successor completing the record was normal. The chapter itself reads like an obituary, praising Moses from a standpoint after his life is complete, which fits a successor's hand. This does not by itself settle larger questions about the composition history of the Pentateuch, on which scholars disagree, but the death notice alone does not disprove Mosaic authorship of the teaching it closes, any more than a biography's final chapter disproves that its subject wrote the letters quoted in it.

Site Assessment

The site's position is that Deuteronomy 34 is best read as a postscript to Moses' work, added after his death, and that it poses no contradiction to the tradition that the teaching of Deuteronomy is Moses'. Wider composition questions are left open here rather than settled by this one chapter.
