The Cursing of the Fig Tree

Passage
The Cursing of the Fig Tree
Reference
Matthew 21:18-22; Mark 11:12-14, 20-25
Difficulty
Jesus, hungry, finds a fig tree in leaf but with no fruit, curses it, and it withers. Readers stumble twice: it seems petty and destructive to kill a tree for being out of season — Mark even notes 'the time of figs was not yet' — and it seems unlike Jesus, who elsewhere feeds crowds and heals, to use power to destroy. Is this a loss of temper, or does the tree mean something more?
Explanations
The widely accepted reading is that this is an acted parable, not a botanical complaint. A fig tree in leaf advertised fruit; finding leaves without figs pictured Israel's temple system — impressive foliage, no fruit — and Mark places the cursing around the temple cleansing, sandwiching the two scenes so each interprets the other. Matthew compresses the timeline, as he often does, so the withering looks immediate. The point is judgment on fruitlessness, not anger at a tree: the tree stands for a people and an institution that bore the appearance of godliness without its fruit. The passage is therefore about the temple's coming judgment, and secondarily about prayer and faith, which both accounts attach to it.
Site Assessment
Site position — read as an acted parable of judgment, the passage is coherent and serious, not a tantrum. The site does not claim this removes all discomfort: a sign-act of judgment is still a judgment, and readers who feel its severity are feeling what the passage intends. What it does not support is reading the tree as a mere victim of bad temper.
