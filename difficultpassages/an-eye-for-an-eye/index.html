An Eye for an Eye

'Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot' sounds like a charter for revenge: injure me and I may injure you back in exactly the same way. That reading clashes with the Bible's own calls to mercy and forgiveness, and with Jesus' teaching to turn the other cheek. Readers wonder whether the law of Moses really licensed personal vengeance, and if so, how it can belong to the same moral world as the rest of Scripture.

Explanations

The scholarly consensus about the original function of this law is that it limited vengeance rather than licensing it. In a world where a lost eye could be repaid with a life, or a clan feud could run for generations, the formula set a ceiling: the penalty must match the injury, no more. It was also a judicial rule, administered by judges, not a permission slip for the injured party to take revenge in person; the surrounding passage is a courtroom scale of compensation for injuries, and later Jewish practice generally applied it as monetary compensation equal to the value of the loss. Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount then addresses personal retaliation and goes beyond the judicial minimum toward non-retaliation, which is a deepening of the ethic, not a correction of a bloodthirsty rule. The law's own point was proportionality and restraint.

Site Assessment

The site's position is that 'eye for eye' was a restraint on revenge, not a command to take it: a judge's rule that punishment must fit the injury and never exceed it. Read in its courtroom context, it points toward the same justice that later teaching carries further into mercy, rather than against it.
