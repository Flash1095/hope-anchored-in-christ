The Long Ending of Mark

Passage
The Long Ending of Mark
Reference
Mark 16:9-20
Difficulty
Mark 16:9-20, with its appearances, commission, and signs such as handling serpents and drinking poison, reads like a fitting ending. But it is absent from the earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts, and early church writers show knowledge of Mark ending at 16:8, with the women fleeing the tomb in fear. Readers ask: is this passage Scripture, was it added later, and can anything built on it — especially the signs in verses 17-18 — be trusted?
Explanations
Textual scholars widely agree the long ending was not part of Mark's original text: it is missing from the two earliest complete Greek Bibles, its vocabulary and style differ from the rest of Mark, and it reads like a summary drawn from the other Gospels and Acts. What is debated is what Mark originally intended: whether his Gospel was meant to end abruptly at 16:8, with fear and silence as a deliberate, unsettling close, or whether an original ending was lost very early and 16:9-20 was supplied to fill the gap. No core Christian doctrine depends on these verses — the resurrection appearances and the commission are fully attested elsewhere — and the signs in verses 17-18 were never a general command to test God with poison or snakes.
Site Assessment
Site position — Mark 16:9-20 should be read as a later addition, honestly marked as such, not as Mark's own ending. Readers should not build doctrine or practice on verses 17-18 alone, and the abrupt ending at 16:8 should be allowed to stand as the text Mark's earliest readers received. This is the site's textual judgment, not a claim that Scripture itself labels the passage.
