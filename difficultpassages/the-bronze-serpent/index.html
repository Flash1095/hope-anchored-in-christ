The Bronze Serpent

Passage
The Bronze Serpent
Reference
Numbers 21:4-9
Difficulty
After Israel complains in the wilderness, God sends fiery serpents, and many die. Then God tells Moses to make a serpent of brass, set it on a pole, and promises that anyone bitten who looks at it will live. The difficulty is sharp: the second commandment forbids making a graven image, and this looks exactly like one, a metal serpent used as a means of healing. Worse, the same object later becomes an idol, Nehushtan, that King Hezekiah has to destroy. Did God command idolatry and then punish it?
Explanations
The distinction drawn by most readers is between an image made and used at God's command as a means of grace, and an image worshipped as a god. The bronze serpent was not worshipped in Numbers 21; it was looked at in obedient trust toward God's promise, and the healing came from God, not from the metal. The object itself was neutral, a God-appointed sign pointing sufferers away from themselves and toward God's mercy. That is also why its later fate makes sense rather than contradicting the story: centuries later, when Israelites were burning incense to it as if it had power in itself, Hezekiah broke it in pieces, calling it a mere thing of brass. The same object was legitimate as a sign and illegitimate as an idol, and the difference lay in how it was treated. The New Testament later uses the lifted-up serpent as a picture of Christ lifted up, which fits the sign reading: the serpent pointed beyond itself.
Site Assessment
The site's position is that the bronze serpent was a commanded sign, not a sanctioned idol: looking at it in faith was obedience to God's word, while burning incense to it later was idolatry. The passage warns against worshipping the means of grace rather than the God who gives it.
