Train Up a Child — a Promise or a Principle?

'Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it.' Parents who raised a child in faith and watched that child walk away hear this as a broken promise, and some blame themselves entirely; others hear it used to guarantee an outcome no parent can control. The difficulty is real: stated flatly, the verse sounds like a guarantee, and experience supplies many exceptions.

Explanations

The widely agreed reading rests on genre. A proverb states a general pattern of how life usually works, not an unconditional divine promise: early training ordinarily shapes a life's direction, and the bent given in childhood tends to persist. It is not a contract that faithful parenting produces faithful adults without fail, and Scripture itself supplies counter-examples of godly parents with wayward children and wayward parents with godly children. The verse's force is therefore encouragement to diligent, early formation, not a verdict on any particular parent or child. A minority pastoral caution adds that 'the way he should go' may also carry the sense of the child's own bent or gifting, which would make the verse about fitting training to the child rather than forcing one mould — but on either reading it remains general wisdom, not a guarantee.

Site Assessment

Site position: Proverbs 22:6 is a general principle about formation, not a promise that can be used to guarantee a child's future faith or to assign blame when a grown child departs. Parents should take its encouragement seriously and its guarantee language loosely.
