Manasseh's Repentance: Kings Silent, Chronicles Full

2 Kings 21 describes Manasseh as Judah's worst king — idolatry, child sacrifice, innocent blood — with no mention of repentance. 2 Chronicles 33 adds that Manasseh was taken to Babylon in fetters, humbled himself, prayed, and was restored to Jerusalem, where he removed idols. Readers ask whether Chronicles invented a happy ending or Kings suppressed one.

Explanations

The two books have different purposes and selections, and neither claims to be exhaustive. Kings, written to explain the exile, stresses the guilt that made judgment inevitable; Chronicles, written to the returned exiles, stresses that even the worst king could repent and be restored — a message its audience needed. An omission in Kings is not a denial, and Chronicles' addition is consistent with the pattern of humbled kings elsewhere. The accounts overlap on Manasseh's crimes and his reign length; they differ in what each chose to include.

Site Assessment

Site assessment: Kings and Chronicles are complementary, not contradictory, on Manasseh. Kings tells why judgment came; Chronicles tells that repentance was still possible, even for him.
