Thou Shalt Not Kill

The sixth commandment in the King James Version reads, 'Thou shalt not kill.' Yet the same law of Moses commands capital punishment, authorises warfare, and permits killing in self-defence and in defence of the home. If all killing is forbidden, then God contradicts his own commandment within a few chapters. If it is not, then the most famous wording of the commandment seems to mislead ordinary readers, and pacifist and non-pacifist Christians alike need to know what it actually forbids.

Explanations

This is a translation point, and it is widely settled. The Hebrew verb used here (ratsach) does not mean killing in general; it means unlawful killing, the taking of innocent life, what modern translations render as 'murder.' The commandment therefore forbids murder, not every act that ends a life: the same legal code that contains it goes on to distinguish deliberate murder from accidental killing, to provide cities of refuge for the accidental killer, and to prescribe death as a judicial penalty for certain crimes. Whether Christians today should support capital punishment or any particular war is a separate moral argument, but it is not decided by pretending this verse bans all killing; the verse's own context in the law shows it never meant that.

Site Assessment

The site's position is that Exodus 20:13 forbids murder, the unlawful taking of innocent life, and that the King James wording 'kill' should be read with that narrower Hebrew sense. The apparent clash with the law's own penalties dissolves once the verb is translated accurately.
