'Why Callest Thou Me Good?'

The rich young ruler addresses Jesus as 'Good Master', and Jesus replies, 'Why callest thou me good? There is none good but one, that is, God.' Readers who believe Jesus is God ask why he would deflect a true title; readers who deny his divinity cite the verse as Jesus' own denial that he is good, and therefore that he is God.

Explanations

The saying is best read as a question, not a denial. Jesus takes the man's word 'good' seriously and presses it: do you realise what you are saying? If only God is good, then calling Jesus good either means recognising who he is, or using a flattering title without understanding it. The context supports this: Jesus does not say 'I am not good,' he asks why the title is given, and then goes on to claim the authority to set the terms of eternal life and discipleship. Matthew's version, 'Why callest thou me good?' followed by 'if thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments,' keeps the same probing tone. Read as a denial, the verse would contradict the Gospels' portrait of a sinless Jesus; read as a probe, it invites the ruler — and the reader — to follow the title to its conclusion.

Site Assessment

Site position — Jesus is probing the title, not disowning it. The site holds that reading because it fits the immediate context and the Gospels as a whole, while acknowledging that the verse, taken alone, can be made to sound like a denial — which is why it must not be taken alone.
