Jacob Steals Isaac's Blessing

Passage
Jacob Steals Isaac's Blessing
Reference
Genesis 27:1-40
Difficulty
Jacob, helped by his mother Rebekah, disguises himself as Esau, lies to his blind father Isaac, and takes the blessing meant for his elder brother. God had already said the elder would serve the younger, but Jacob gets there by fraud, and the blessing, once spoken, cannot be taken back. Readers stumble twice over: first, does God reward cheating, since Jacob goes on to become the father of Israel? And second, does the story make deception acceptable when it serves a promised outcome?
Explanations
Commentators widely agree on two points that keep the story from endorsing the trick. First, the narrative never praises the deception: Isaac trembles when he discovers it, Esau weeps, and Jacob pays for years, fleeing for his life, being tricked in turn by Laban over his own wives, and living in fear of Esau's revenge. The consequences are woven through the following chapters as the story's own judgment on the method. Second, God's purpose and human scheming are kept distinct: the promise that the elder would serve the younger was given before the twins were born, and the story shows God working his purpose out even through flawed people, not because of their flaws. Rebekah's earlier scheme, Esau's casual sale of his birthright, and Isaac's attempt to bless the son he favoured are all shown as human grasping around a promise God had already made.
Site Assessment
The site's position is that Genesis 27 describes deception and its bitter costs; it does not commend it. God's promise to Jacob stands in spite of the trick, not because the trick was good, and the chapters that follow are best read as the price Jacob paid for taking the blessing by hand rather than waiting for God to give it.
