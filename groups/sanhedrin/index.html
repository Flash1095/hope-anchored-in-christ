The Sanhedrin

Group
The Sanhedrin
Also Called
The Council; The Great Sanhedrin
Period
Second Temple period, especially the first century AD
Origin
The Sanhedrin was the supreme Jewish council in Jerusalem, rooted in the biblical pattern of elders judging the people and developed under Persian, Greek, and Roman rule into a court of seventy-one under the high priest. Under Rome its powers were real but limited: it governed religious life and local affairs, while capital punishment required the Roman governor.
Beliefs
The council was not a party with one theology. It mixed Sadducean chief priests and elders with Pharisaic scribes, so its members disagreed sharply — notably over the resurrection — and decisions came from debate and majority vote rather than shared doctrine.
Practices
It met in Jerusalem, tried religious and civil cases, issued rulings, sent messengers, and policed the Temple. Its sessions could be formal or hurried, and the Gospel accounts show it acting at night in Jesus' case, a detail that has long been debated by scholars.
Role
The Sanhedrin was the highest Jewish authority most Jews would ever meet: court, council, and senate in one. Ordinary people looked to it for justice and for the boundaries of faithful life, and Rome tolerated it as the instrument of local self-government.
Relationship To Jesus
The Sanhedrin condemned Jesus after a night hearing and handed him to Pilate, pressing for crucifixion. It then tried the apostles, imprisoned and flogged them, and stoned Stephen. Yet its own members were divided: Nicodemus urged a fair hearing, Joseph of Arimathea dissented, and Gamaliel — named in Acts though he has no profile on this site — advised restraint.
Key Members
Caiaphas; Annas; Nicodemus; Joseph of Arimathea
Scripture
Matthew 26:57-68; Mark 14:53-65; John 11:47-53; Acts 4:5-22; Acts 23:1-10
Summary
The Sanhedrin was Jerusalem's supreme council of seventy-one — chief priests, elders, and scribes under the high priest — the court that judged Jesus and then tried to silence his apostles, while remaining deeply divided within itself.
