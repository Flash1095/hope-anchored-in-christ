Tax Collectors

Group
Tax Collectors
Also Called
Publicans
Period
The first century AD under Roman rule
Origin
Tax collectors were Jews employed to gather Rome's taxes: customs duties, tolls, and land taxes, often through a system that let collectors keep whatever they took above their quota. They worked for the occupying power and grew rich from their neighbors' pockets, which made them the most despised class in Jewish society.
Beliefs
As a class they held no party theology. Their religion was practical: money, security, and survival under Rome. Many were observant Jews in private, but their trade put them outside respectable religious society.
Practices
They sat at toll booths and tax offices, assessed goods and travelers, seized property for unpaid dues, and kept the books for Rome's revenue. Some, like Zacchaeus, were chief collectors running whole districts.
Role
Tax collectors were the collaborators everyone loved to hate: religiously unclean by association, socially exiled from synagogue respectability, and lumped in speech with 'sinners.' A Pharisee thanking God he was not like a tax collector was praying a prayer his hearers understood instantly.
Relationship To Jesus
Jesus scandalized respectable religion by eating with tax collectors, calling Matthew from his toll booth to follow him, and staying at Zacchaeus's house. His parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector makes the despised collector the model of justified prayer, and his table fellowship with them became one of the main charges against him.
Key Members
Matthew; Zacchaeus
Scripture
Matthew 9:9-13; Luke 18:9-14; Luke 19:1-10; Mark 2:13-17; Matthew 21:31-32
Summary
Tax collectors were the Jews who gathered Rome's taxes for profit — despised as collaborators and 'sinners,' yet the very people Jesus ate with, called, and held up as models of repentance.
Related Topics
Money; Repentance; Grace; Forgiveness; Hypocrisy
Related Books
Matthew; Mark; Luke
