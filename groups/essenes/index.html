Essenes

Group
Essenes
Period
Second Temple period, roughly the second century BC through the first century AD
Origin
The Essenes are known not from Scripture, which never names them, but from the Jewish writers Josephus and Philo and the Roman Pliny. They were a separatist Jewish movement that withdrew from ordinary society into disciplined communities, prizing purity, shared property, and strict observance.
Beliefs
Essenes shared the Pharisees' belief in providence and the afterlife but pushed further: rigorous purity, communal ownership, daily washings, white garments, celibacy in some communities, and a calendar and discipline of their own. They regarded the Jerusalem Temple and priesthood as corrupt.
Practices
They lived in ordered communities with common meals, common property, strict admission after years of testing, daily prayer and study, and baths for ritual purity. Some lived in towns; the best-known settlement tradition places a community at Qumran near the Dead Sea.
Role
Essenes stood apart from Jewish public life. They did not offer Temple sacrifices in the usual way, did not marry in some communities, and kept a low profile — respected for discipline, but a movement most Jews knew of rather than joined.
Relationship To Jesus
Jesus never meets an Essene in the Gospels, and no Essene is named in Scripture. Their importance for readers is background: they show how seriously some Jews took purity and separation, and they are the leading — but debated — candidates for the community behind the Dead Sea Scrolls. Whether Qumran was Essene is a genuine scholarly debate, not a settled fact, and this entry does not settle it.
Summary
The Essenes were a separatist Jewish movement of strict purity and shared life, known from Josephus, Philo, and Pliny rather than from Scripture — the disciplined outsiders whose possible link to the Dead Sea Scrolls remains debated.
