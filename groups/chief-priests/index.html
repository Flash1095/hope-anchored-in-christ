Chief Priests

Group
Chief Priests
Period
Second Temple period, especially the first century AD
Origin
The chief priests were the high priest's circle: former high priests, the heads of the leading priestly families, and the Temple's senior officers. Drawn largely from a few wealthy aristocratic families, they held office through influence and, under Rome, through political appointment as much as by birth.
Beliefs
As a class they were aligned with the Sadducees: they accepted the written Law, were skeptical of the resurrection and the oral tradition, and valued order, Temple worship, and good relations with Rome over popular religious movements.
Practices
They ran the Temple: its treasury, its sacrifices, its police, and its daily schedule. They negotiated with the Roman prefect, collected Temple revenue, and guarded the sanctuary's sanctity — including the money-changing and sacrifice-selling that Jesus disrupted.
Role
The chief priests were the Jewish aristocracy of Jerusalem — wealthy, powerful, and close to Rome. Ordinary Jews respected the Temple they ran but often resented the families who profited from it, and the chief priests guarded their position jealously against popular preachers and movements.
Relationship To Jesus
The chief priests were Jesus' most persistent institutional opponents. They questioned his authority, plotted his arrest, paid Judas, ran the night hearing, and pressed Pilate for crucifixion. After the resurrection they tried to suppress the apostles' preaching and bribed the tomb's guards to spread a cover story.
Key Members
Caiaphas; Annas
Scripture
Matthew 26:3-5; Matthew 27:1-2; Mark 14:1-2; John 18:3; Acts 9:1-2
Summary
The chief priests were the Temple's ruling aristocracy — the high priest's circle of wealthy priestly families who ran Jerusalem's religious life, negotiated with Rome, and led the opposition that handed Jesus over to Pilate.
