Contextualization

Principle
Contextualization
Category
Principle
Summary
Contextualization is the work of bridging an ancient text to a new culture without changing its meaning: expressing the same truth in forms, language, and examples the hearers actually understand. It is the step after application — application asks what the text means for me, and contextualization asks how that meaning is faithfully carried into a different time, place, or culture. Done well, it preserves the message while changing the packaging; done badly, it either keeps foreign packaging that nobody understands or alters the message to suit the audience.
How To Apply
Separate the timeless truth from its original cultural packaging before you carry it anywhere: what did this command or promise mean in its world, and what is the equivalent faithful expression in mine? Test every bridge in both directions — does it still say what the text said, and does it actually land where the hearers live? Where a practice was culture-bound (greetings, food customs, dress), translate the principle; where the text gives a moral command, do not translate it away.
Scripture Example
1 Corinthians 9:19-23 shows Paul becoming all things to all people — adapting his approach to Jews and Gentiles alike — while refusing to change the gospel itself. Acts 15 shows the church deciding the same question institutionally: Gentile believers were not required to adopt Jewish cultural packaging in order to belong to God's people, because the gospel, not the packaging, is what saves.
Common Mistake
Two opposite failures: under-contextualizing, so the message stays locked in ancient forms and sounds foreign, and over-contextualizing, so the message is reshaped until it merely echoes the audience's own values. Faithful contextualization changes the form, never the truth.
Scripture
1 Corinthians 9:19-23; Acts 15:1-29
