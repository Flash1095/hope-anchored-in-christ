Historical-Critical Method

Principle
Historical-Critical Method
Category
Method
Summary
The historical-critical method is the academic study of the Bible's history: source criticism (what sources lie behind a text), form criticism (what oral forms it took), and redaction criticism (how editors shaped it). It has produced genuine historical insight, and it is contested: some practitioners work from naturalistic presuppositions that rule out the supernatural in advance, while evangelical scholars use its tools within a high view of Scripture — so the method's value depends heavily on the presuppositions brought to it.
How To Apply
Use its tools for background — sources, forms, and editing can illuminate a text's setting — while keeping the text's own claims in view. Ask of any reconstruction: does it explain the text as it stands, or does it replace the text with a theory about the text?
Scripture Example
Luke 1:1-4 — Luke himself describes gathering sources and composing 'an orderly account,' which is why source questions are legitimate: the evangelists worked with material. The debate is not whether sources existed but whether reconstructions may override the finished Gospel's own testimony.
Common Mistake
Letting a reconstruction replace the text's claims — so that what the text says matters less than what critics suppose happened behind it — or dismissing all historical study as unbelief.
Scripture
Luke 1:1-4; John 20:30-31; 2 Timothy 3:16
