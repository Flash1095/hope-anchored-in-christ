Textual Criticism

Principle
Textual Criticism
Category
Term
Summary
Textual criticism (lower criticism) is the discipline of recovering the original wording of the biblical text from its thousands of manuscripts. Scribes made occasional errors and additions; by comparing manuscripts, scholars reconstruct what was most likely written, and translations note the significant variants in footnotes.
How To Apply
Read footnotes about variants without alarm: the vast majority are spelling or word-order differences, and no central doctrine rests on a disputed reading. Where a variant matters (Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11), note what the footnotes say and let the rest of Scripture carry the doctrine.
Scripture Example
Mark 16:9-20 — most translations bracket or footnote the longer ending of Mark because the earliest manuscripts lack it. The passage's disputed status does not threaten the resurrection, which the rest of the New Testament attests abundantly; it shows textual criticism doing its honest work in public.
Common Mistake
Concluding from variants that the text is unreliable — when in fact the manuscript wealth lets scholars recover the text with great confidence — or ignoring variants entirely and treating every printed word as equally certain.
Scripture
2 Timothy 3:16; Revelation 22:18-19; Mark 16:9-20
