Translation Philosophy

Principle
Translation Philosophy
Category
Principle
Summary
Translation philosophy is the set of decisions a Bible translation makes about how closely to follow the original wording versus how freely to restate the meaning. At one end are formal-equivalence translations, which stay as close as possible to the words and structures of Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek; at the other are dynamic- or functional-equivalence translations, which prioritize expressing the same meaning in natural, modern language. Most translations sit somewhere between the two poles, and no translation is purely one or the other.
How To Apply
When a passage puzzles you, compare a more word-for-word translation with a more thought-for-thought one: agreement between them usually means the meaning is secure, while differences flag a place where the original is ambiguous or idiomatic. Remember that every translation is already an interpretation at the points where the original languages do not map neatly onto English, so lean on the original-language tools in Word Studies for key terms rather than building doctrine on a single English wording.
Scripture Example
Nehemiah 8:8 describes the Levites reading from the Law of God, making it clear and giving the sense so the people could understand — translation as faithful mediation, not mere word-swapping. A modern example: a formal translation keeps Paul's long, layered sentences intact, while a dynamic one breaks them into shorter sentences; both are translating the same Greek, but they make different trade-offs between precision and readability.
Common Mistake
Assuming your preferred translation is the original, or dismissing a translation from the other end of the spectrum as unfaithful. The mistake is treating translation philosophy as a loyalty test instead of asking what each approach gains and loses.
Scripture
Nehemiah 8:8; 2 Timothy 3:16-17
