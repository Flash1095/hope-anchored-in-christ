Reader-Response Reading

Reader-Response Reading

Summary

Reader-response reading locates meaning in the reader's encounter with the text: what a passage does to its readers, how communities receive it, and how different readers hear it differently. Its advocates value attention to reception and application; its critics, including most evangelical interpreters, argue that making the reader the authority dissolves authorial intent — the site's own position, stated plainly, is that meaning is anchored in what the author intended, while readers genuinely differ in how they receive and apply that meaning.

How To Apply

Use reader-response insights where they belong: notice how a text lands on different audiences, and let reception history sharpen your reading. But keep the question of meaning separate from the question of response — what a text does to you is not the same as what it says.

Common Mistake

Treating the reader's response as the text's meaning, so that one passage means opposite things to different readers and no reading can ever be wrong.

Details

Category: Method
Scripture Example: Luke 10:25-37 — the parable of the Good Samaritan lands differently on a priest, a lawyer, and a Samaritan in the story, and on different readers today. Noticing those receptions enriches the reading; it does not license making the parable mean whatever each reader feels.
Scripture: 2 Peter 1:20-21; Nehemiah 8:8; Luke 10:25-37
