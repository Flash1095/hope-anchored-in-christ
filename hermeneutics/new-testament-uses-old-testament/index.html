How the New Testament Uses the Old Testament

Principle
How the New Testament Uses the Old Testament
Category
Principle
Summary
New Testament writers draw on the Old Testament in several distinct ways: direct quotation introduced by a formula such as 'it is written,' allusion that echoes older wording without quoting it, and typological fulfillment in which a person, event, or institution foreshadows a later reality. Recognizing which kind is in play matters, because a direct quotation used as proof functions differently from an echo that colors a passage or a type that finds its climax in Christ.
How To Apply
When you meet an Old Testament reference in the New Testament, look up the original passage in its own context first, then ask how the New Testament writer is using it: as direct evidence, as an echo, or as a fulfilled pattern. Do not assume every quotation means the Old Testament passage had only the New Testament event in view; many quoted passages had a genuine meaning for their first audience and a fuller significance the New Testament draws out.
Scripture Example
Matthew 1:22-23 quotes Isaiah 7:14 with a fulfillment formula to present Jesus' birth as the climax of God's promised sign. Luke 24:27 shows Jesus himself explaining from Moses and all the Prophets the things concerning himself, modeling a reading of the whole Old Testament as pointing toward him. Romans 4 reasons from Abraham's story in Genesis 15 to argue about faith and righteousness for all peoples.
Common Mistake
Treating every New Testament echo as a direct prediction, or the opposite error of treating New Testament use of the Old as arbitrary. Both miss that the apostles read the Old Testament as one unfolding story with real continuity and real development.
Scripture
Matthew 1:22-23; Luke 24:27; Romans 4:1-12
