Allegorical Method

Principle
Allegorical Method
Category
Method
Summary
The allegorical method reads beneath the literal sense for hidden spiritual meanings, treating persons, objects, and events as symbols of deeper truths. It flourished in the Alexandrian school (notably Origen) and in the medieval fourfold sense, which sought literal, allegorical, moral, and heavenly meanings in every text. Its defenders value its Christ-centered depth; its critics note that without controls, a text can be made to mean whatever the interpreter wishes.
How To Apply
Use the allegorical tradition as background, not as a license: note where the New Testament itself draws spiritual meanings (Galatians 4) and distinguish apostolic use from later free allegory. Test any proposed hidden meaning against the text's own grammar, context, and genre.
Scripture Example
Galatians 4:21-31 — Paul himself reads the story of Hagar and Sarah allegorically, as two covenants. The passage shows that spiritual reading has apostolic precedent, while also showing its limits: Paul's reading is argued from the text's covenant logic, not imposed from outside it.
Common Mistake
Finding meanings only the interpreter can see, detached from the text's words and context — so that one passage yields opposite allegories in different hands and authorial intent disappears.
Scripture
Galatians 4:21-31; 1 Corinthians 10:1-11; Luke 10:25-37
